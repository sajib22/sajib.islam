RF engineer in Toronto. Engineer-in-Training with APEGS, working toward P.Eng.

The long version

I’m an RF engineer at Genwave Technologies in Toronto, and my work is almost entirely in-building: distributed antenna systems, Private 5G and Cisco URWB networks for large venues. That means the design package and link budget up front, then commissioning, CW and PIM testing, walk testing, and the optimization that moves a system from installed to actually meeting its engineered targets.

Before Canada I spent fifteen years on the macro side. Five of them were at Nokia, leading LTE and 5G optimization and doing the multi-vendor KPI and counter mapping that proves a network still behaves the same after a swap. Before that I planned and optimized 4G and 3G for MTN Irancell in Tehran, running a transition across more than two thousand sites. I started out at Grameenphone, Ericsson and Huawei in Dhaka, where I designed more than a hundred in-building systems and ran a 250-site IBS vendor swap.

That background is why I tend to read an in-building problem as a network problem first and a cabling problem second. A venue with poor coverage is rarely just a venue with poor coverage — it is a donor that was never characterised properly, or a neighbour list nobody updated, or a KPI target somebody agreed to without checking whether the physics allowed it.

The other half of my work is making the analysis faster. I write Python that turns raw measurement data into finished reports, and Power BI models that turn counter dumps into a ranked list of things to fix. Fifteen years of assembling that kind of thing by hand is a good reason to stop doing it by hand.

I’ve worked across Bangladesh, Iran, Myanmar and now Canada, mostly with teams where English was nobody’s first language, including mine. That turns out to be good training for the part of engineering that is actually about getting people to agree on what the numbers mean.
