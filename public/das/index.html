All About DAS

All About DAS

A distributed antenna system is what you build when a building defeats the network outside it. This is the working explanation I give clients — what a DAS is, how the signal actually gets to a phone on the eleventh floor, and which type to use when.

The Signal Path, End to End

Distributed Antenna System (DAS)

A DAS extends wireless coverage inside buildings where the outdoor macro network cannot reach. Instead of relying on one high-power source, it splits the signal across many low-power antennas spread through every floor.

Where the signal comes from: either an on-site carrier base station (BTS) for a full off-air-independent system, or a rooftop donor antenna feeding a bi-directional amplifier (BDA) that repeats the macro network indoors. Both terminate at the head end.

How it is distributed: the head end converts RF to optical and sends it up the riser on fibre. Remote units on each floor convert it back to RF and drive the ceiling antennas over coax. Because the backbone is fibre, the same head end can reach neighbouring buildings through conversion units at their base — which is how a campus is covered from one source.

Why it matters: concrete cores, low-E glass and deep floor plates block most outdoor signal. A properly engineered DAS delivers reliable voice, data and emergency-service coverage everywhere in the venue, from the parking levels to the top floor.

What Is a DAS?

A distributed antenna system replaces one distant, high-power signal source with many low-power antennas spread through a building. The macro network outside is designed to cover streets and open ground. Once that signal has to pass through a concrete core, a basement wall or modern low-emissivity glass, it arrives too weak and too noisy to carry a call — coated glass alone can cost 25 dB or more.

A DAS solves that by moving the antenna closer to the user. Instead of asking one signal to punch through the building envelope, the system carries it inside over cable or fibre and re-radiates it floor by floor. The result is even coverage at low power, which also means less interference back into the macro network and better battery life on every device connected to it.

The distinction worth holding on to: a DAS is a distribution system, not a source of capacity. It takes signal from somewhere — a base station or the macro network — and spreads it. What it cannot do is create capacity that the source never had.

How Does a DAS Work?

Every DAS has three stages: a signal source, a distribution backbone, and the antennas that finally radiate.

1. The source. Either an on-site base station (BTS or small cell) fed from the carrier’s network, or a rooftop donor antenna pointed at a nearby macro site. The donor route feeds a bi-directional amplifier (BDA) that repeats the outdoor network indoors. An on-site base station gives dedicated capacity; a donor and BDA share whatever the macro cell already has, which is cheaper but caps what the building can carry.

2. The head end. Sources are combined here, and in an active system the RF is converted to optical. Fibre is what makes reach practical — it runs hundreds of metres with negligible loss, where coax of the same length would lose most of the signal to cable attenuation.

3. Distribution and radiation. The backbone runs up a riser to remote units on each floor, which convert back to RF and drive ceiling antennas over short coax tails. In a passive system there is no conversion at all: couplers and splitters divide one source across the antennas, losing a predictable amount at each split.

A DAS is bi-directional throughout. The uplink — the phone talking back — is usually the harder direction to engineer, because a handset transmits at a fraction of the power the network does, and uplink noise accumulated across every remote unit lands back at one receiver.

Types of DAS

Passive DAS

Coax, couplers and splitters only — no powered components after the source. Cheap, extremely reliable and effectively maintenance-free, but loss accumulates with distance, so it suits smaller buildings and shorter cable runs.

Active DAS

Fibre from a head end to powered remote units on each floor. Handles large and tall buildings, multiple operators and multiple bands, with per-remote control over power. More expensive, and it needs power and monitoring at every remote.

Hybrid DAS

Fibre backbone to floor-level remotes, then passive coax to the antennas on that floor. The common compromise in practice: most of the reach of active, with far fewer powered devices to install and maintain.

Digital DAS

The signal is digitised at the head end and transported over structured cabling or fibre. Cleanest performance and the easiest to reconfigure remotely, which matters most on large multi-operator estates.

Off-Air (Repeater) DAS

Fed by a donor antenna and BDA rather than a base station. Fast and comparatively cheap, but it inherits the macro cell’s capacity and needs careful isolation between donor and service antennas or it will oscillate.

Public Safety DAS

A separate system for first-responder radio, on its own bands and to its own codes. Usually mandated by the local fire authority, with battery backup, survivable cabling and annual testing that commercial systems are not held to.

The Parts, and What Each One Does

  • BTS — base transceiver station. The carrier’s own radio equipment, giving the building dedicated capacity.
  • Donor antenna — a directional rooftop antenna aimed at a macro site, used when no on-site base station is available.
  • BDA — bi-directional amplifier. Amplifies both directions of an off-air feed. Its gain is limited by isolation from the donor.
  • Head end — combines sources, sets levels, and converts RF to optical in an active system.
  • Riser — the vertical backbone, fibre or coax, running the height of the building.
  • Remote unit — converts back to RF and amplifies for its floor. One per floor is typical.
  • Expansion unit — extends the same head end into adjacent buildings, which is how a campus is covered from one source.
  • Coupler and splitter — divide signal between antennas in a known, calculable ratio.
  • Service antenna — the ceiling antenna that finally radiates, usually omnidirectional indoors.

When a Building Needs One

The usual triggers are structural rather than electronic: large floor plates where the centre is far from any window, below-grade parking and plant rooms, concrete cores and shear walls, low-E glass, and any building where a fire code requires first-responder radio coverage in every stairwell and basement.

Occupancy matters as much as construction. A stadium or a transit hub may have perfectly good coverage and still fail, because thousands of devices are contending for one cell. That is a capacity problem, and the fix is more sectors or more sources — distributing a signal that has already run out of capacity does not help.

Designing and Proving One

Design starts with a survey and a link budget. Every element between source and antenna — cable loss, splitter and coupler loss, connectors — is accounted for, so the power arriving at each antenna is a calculated number rather than a hope. That budget drives antenna count and placement, which is then modelled against the floor plans to predict signal level and quality across every space.

Proving it is a separate discipline. Commissioning verifies each remote and antenna run; CW testing measures actual path loss against the prediction; PIM testing catches passive intermodulation from a bad connector or corroded joint, which shows up as unexplained uplink noise; and walk testing records the delivered signal level, quality and throughput across the building. EMF exposure is validated against the applicable limit — Safety Code 6 in Canada. The close-out pack is what the carrier accepts, and it is the difference between a system that is installed and one that is proven.
